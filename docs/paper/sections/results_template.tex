\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Two-tier evaluation overview}
We benchmark every agent at \emph{both} the Standard tier (12 families,
near-ceiling difficulty) and the T\textsubscript{extreme} tier (5
families with $\delta \in [0.97, 1.0]$). The Standard tier targets 240
tasks per agent (20 per family $\times$ 12) and the
T\textsubscript{extreme} tier targets 40 tasks per agent (8 per family
$\times$ 5); realised per-agent $N$ varies because of provider-side
refusals and multi-seed pooling on Pareto-essentials --- both are
disclosed in \autoref{tab:two-tier}. Headline Spearman and Pareto-frontier
flags use a pre-registered minimum-$N$ filter ($N \ge 100$ Standard,
$N \ge 20$ T\textsubscript{extreme}). Bootstrap 95\,\% CIs ($B = 10^4$,
percentile) are reported for every metric. \autoref{tab:two-tier} shows
the side-by-side leaderboard; \autoref{tab:headline} ranks Standard-tier
agents by net payoff.

\paragraph{H1: rank by accuracy $\ne$ rank by calibration $\ne$ rank by
payoff.}
Spearman $\rho$ on the $K = 15$ agents with $N \geq 100$ on the Standard
tier:
$\rho_{\text{acc, ECE}} = -0.93$,
$\rho_{\text{acc, payoff}} = +0.76$,
$\rho_{\text{ECE, payoff}} = -0.67$.
The strong negative acc--ECE correlation reflects that more accurate
models are typically also better calibrated. The acc--payoff correlation
($\rho = 0.76$) is high but not unity: the best-by-accuracy and
best-by-ECE model is \texttt{google/gemini-3-flash-preview} (acc $0.970$,
ECE $0.027$), whereas the best-by-payoff model is a sibling
\texttt{google/gemini-3.1-pro-preview} (payoff $37{,}813$ vs $23{,}800$
for the Flash model and $24{,}824$ for \texttt{claude-opus-4.7}). The
two Gemini variants differ in commitment policy and in per-task cost ---
which the payoff axis captures and the accuracy axis does not. Combined
with the H4 hard-tier collapse below, the orderings induced by each
axis are demonstrably different at the first-place level.

\paragraph{H2: reasoning mode raises conditional accuracy and net payoff
but does not uniformly increase strategic abstention.}
We compare reasoning-tuned variants against their non-reasoning siblings
on the T\textsubscript{extreme} tier:
\texttt{deepseek-r1} vs.\ \texttt{deepseek-v3.2},
and \texttt{qwen3-235b-a22b-thinking-2507} vs.\ \texttt{qwen3-32b}.
On the DeepSeek family the reasoning variant abstains far more
(\texttt{deepseek-r1} 86\,\% \textsc{Pass} rate vs.\
\texttt{deepseek-v3.2} 4\,\%); on the Qwen family the direction
\emph{reverses} (\texttt{qwen3-235b-thinking} 26\,\% vs.\
\texttt{qwen3-32b} 48\,\%), because the smaller 32B model PASSes from
inability rather than calibrated abstention. The robust signal is in
\emph{conditional accuracy}: reasoning variants are markedly higher
(\texttt{deepseek-r1} $82.4\,\%$ vs.\ \texttt{deepseek-v3.2} $51.0\,\%$;
\texttt{qwen3-235b-thinking} $83.1\,\%$ vs.\ \texttt{qwen3-32b}
$67.3\,\%$) and so is net payoff (DeepSeek $2755$ vs.\ $550$; Qwen
$4825$ vs.\ $1588$). The original hypothesis ``reasoning over-commits''
is \textbf{rejected} on net payoff for both families, but the
intuitive mechanism (``reasoning leads to more strategic abstention'')
\textbf{holds only for DeepSeek, not for Qwen}. The unified statement
the data support is: \emph{extended thinking improves conditional
accuracy enough that, even when the variant commits less, net payoff
goes up}.

\paragraph{H3: open-weight Pareto-essentials match closed frontier on
the cost-payoff axis, not the ECE-payoff axis.}
The T\textsubscript{extreme} tier $(\ECE, \mathcal{P})$ Pareto frontier
(after the $N \ge 20$ minimum-$N$ filter) contains two closed-source
agents: \texttt{openai/gpt-5.2} (ECE $0.025$, payoff $10{,}500$, $N$$=$$71$)
and \texttt{openai/gpt-5} (ECE $0.019$, payoff $5{,}274$, $N$$=$$36$).
The closest open-weight challenger, \texttt{qwen3-235b-thinking}
(ECE $0.091$, payoff $4{,}825$), is dominated on $(\ECE, \mathcal{P})$
by both. \emph{However}, on the $(\text{cost}, \mathcal{P})$ frontier
Qwen3-235B-thinking delivers $4{,}825$ at \$1.00 vs.\ \texttt{gpt-5.2}'s
$10{,}500$ at \$4.07 --- $4{,}825/\$ = 4{,}825$ payoff-per-dollar vs.\
$2{,}580$ payoff-per-dollar --- so the cost-conscious open-weight
deployment claim survives even though the strict $(\ECE, \mathcal{P})$
claim does not. The open vs closed gap on raw accuracy (Standard tier
$91\,\%$ closed-mean vs.\ $87\,\%$ open-mean) translates to a small but
real ECE gap on T\textsubscript{extreme} where the closed-source
reasoning models pull away.

\subsection{Cross-family calibration transfer}
We compute the Spearman rank-correlation of per-family ECE rankings
against the panel-average ranking. A high $\rho$ means calibration is a
\emph{global} trait; a low $\rho$ means it is family-specific. On the
matched Standard-tier $K = 15$ ($N \ge 100$) panel, the median per-agent
$\rho$ across the 12 families is $0.55$ with inter-quartile range
$[0.40, 0.72]$. Three agents have $\rho > 0.85$ (calibration is a global
trait for \texttt{claude-opus-4.7}, \texttt{gpt-5},
\texttt{gemini-3.1-pro-preview}); five agents have $\rho < 0.30$
(calibration is strongly family-specific for the cheaper variants such
as \texttt{llama-4-scout}, \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano},
\texttt{gemini-3.1-flash-lite}). This is direct empirical support for
our Pareto rather than scalar leaderboard choice: a model that calibrates
well on knowledge may calibrate poorly on multimodal --- the right
choice depends on the deployment mix.

\subsection{H4: hard-tier accuracy collapse}
The T\textsubscript{extreme} tier shows that the Standard-tier
near-ceiling effect was a difficulty-floor artefact. On the matched
$N$-filtered Standard panel ($K = 15$, $N \ge 100$) the median accuracy
drops from $87.0\,\%$ (Standard) to $60.0\,\%$
(T\textsubscript{extreme}, $N \ge 20$) --- a $27.0$-percentage-point
fall --- while the median ECE \emph{worsens} by $+0.123$ (Standard
$0.110$, T\textsubscript{extreme} $0.233$, a $2.12\times$ inflation).
Specific dramatic drops include
\texttt{claude-opus-4.7} ($90.0\,\% \to 63.6\,\%$, $\Delta = 26.4$\,pp),
\texttt{deepseek-v3.2} ($84.6\,\% \to 51.0\,\%$),
and \texttt{llama-4-scout} ($83.1\,\% \to 17.0\,\%$). Models that
maintain a fixed high-confidence reporting policy
($\hat P \approx 1$ on every task) incur a payoff penalty roughly
proportional to their hard-tier accuracy deficit. The Pareto leaders on
Standard vs.\ T\textsubscript{extreme} differ
(\autoref{tab:two-tier}): \texttt{openai/gpt-5.2} takes the new
T\textsubscript{extreme} Pareto-frontier crown with $98.6\,\%$ committed
accuracy and ECE $0.025$ at \$10{,}500 net payoff (committing on $34\,\%$
of tasks), while \texttt{openai/gpt-5} achieves perfect ($100\,\%$)
conditional accuracy via $86\,\%$ strategic abstention --- the same
vendor at the same compute scale produces two genuinely different
deployment profiles. The right ranking depends firmly on the deployment
regime.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The full headline table reproduces from the released seed triple
(\verb|seed=42|, \verb|cycle_seed=42|, \verb|market_seed=42|) plus the
Pareto-essential multi-seed rotation (\verb|cycle_seed| $\in
\{42, 7, 1729\}$ for \texttt{gpt-5}, \texttt{gpt-5.2},
\texttt{claude-opus-4.7}, \texttt{deepseek-r1}) by running
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\small\ttfamily, frame=single]
make install
make run-paper            # Standard tier, full panel, seed=42
make run-paper-hard       # T_extreme tier, seeds 42 + multi-seed essentials
make finalize-paper       # regenerate figures + tables + paper text
make paper                # compile PDF
\end{lstlisting}
End-to-end wall-clock on a single workstation with concurrency$=$8:
approximately 90 minutes for the Standard-tier full panel and a further
2--3 hours for the T\textsubscript{extreme} multi-seed Pareto-essentials.
Total inference spend on OpenRouter: \$34.65 USD across the full
two-tier matrix (Standard \$25.58 + T\textsubscript{extreme}
multi-seed \$9.07).
